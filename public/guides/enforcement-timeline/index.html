From the first notice to a closed case.

Most cases offer a chance to fix the property. Know which stage you are in, because a courtesy warning, a hearing notice and a recorded lien require different responses.

By Fix My ViolationLast reviewed: October 7, 2026

The short answer

Find the compliance date and hearing date on your notice. Contact the assigned officer, arrange the work, and request written confirmation after reinspection. Do not assume every case follows every step below.

A common administrative path

  1. Complaint or proactive inspection

    An officer inspects the condition. A case may start without a neighbor complaint. Florida generally requires a complainant’s name and address, with statutory exceptions for imminent threats.

  2. Courtesy contact, when used

    A door hanger or warning may explain what to correct. A separate courtesy warning is not guaranteed in every case.

  3. Notice of violation and compliance date

    The notice identifies the rule and corrective action. Under § 162.06(2), an ordinary Part I case generally provides a reasonable time to correct; it does not establish one universal grass-cutting deadline.

  4. Reinspection

    The officer checks the cited conditions. If the work is incomplete, the case can move forward. Send evidence promptly rather than waiting for the officer to happen past.

  5. Hearing and written order

    A board or Special Magistrate considers evidence and may set a compliance date and fine consequences. A hearing can still occur after a late cleanup.

  6. Daily fine, if imposed

    For an ordinary first violation, fines under § 162.09 can run for days beyond the ordered compliance date. Repeat violations can be treated differently.

  7. Recorded lien and potential collection

    A recorded fine order can become a lien. Nonpayment can lead to further collection, potentially including a foreclosure action subject to legal limits. See the lien guide.

Government mowing is a separate possible branch

Local abatement procedures can allow the city or county to arrange cleanup and charge the responsible property or owner. Do not assume a government mower must wait for the entire administrative-hearing sequence above. The applicable local ordinance and abatement notice control.

Pinellas County explicitly warns that it may hire a mowing or debris-removal contractor and place an interest-accruing lien for the cost. An abatement bill and an administrative fine are different charges; resolving one may leave the other outstanding.

A civil citation or notice to appear in court is another possible path. It has its own instructions. Do not treat a citation as merely another reminder to mow.

When the process can move faster

Section 162.06(3)–(4) makes different provisions for repeat violations and serious threats or irreversible conditions. In a repeat case, the officer need not give another reasonable correction period before requesting a hearing. A prior violation at a different location may matter under the statutory definition.

Do not count on a fresh warning each time grass grows back. Start recurring maintenance while the property is still in good condition.

What to do at your stage

Match your action to the document
What you receivedYour next step
Warning or violation noticeConfirm the required work and deadline; request any extension early.
Notice of hearingPrepare evidence and attend unless the hearing is formally canceled.
Order or fines runningArrange compliance, confirm the effective compliance date and obtain an updated balance.
Lien or abatement billGet a payoff and ask about reduction or release requirements separately from cleanup.
Court citation or summonsFollow the court response instructions promptly and seek legal advice if needed.

A booking request, invoice or pending extension request does not pause enforcement. Get any changed deadline or canceled hearing confirmed by the authorized office.
